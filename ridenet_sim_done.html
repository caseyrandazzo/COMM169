    Powered by Whom?
    What the Daniel V. story tells us about a company selling a story it cannot sustain.
    By Caroline Stahl · May 18, 2026

    Daniel V. had been driving for fourteen hours when he fell asleep at a red light on the FDR and rear-ended the car in front of him. No one was hurt. By midnight, his passenger Kira Brooklyn's account of what he said after the crash had been seen four million times on X, and the police report from the scene had been leaked to multiple outlets. By the next morning, what Daniel told her about the Streak Bonus and his fourteen-hour shift had become a question for the whole company.

    Kira Brooklyn, a passenger in the back seat of Daniel's car when the crash occurred, posted to X within an hour describing what happened. She wrote that Daniel had just rear-ended the car in front of him at a red light, that he was visibly disoriented for a moment, that he confirmed no one was hurt and apologized to the driver he hit, and that when she asked him why he was still working at hour fourteen, he answered before saying he was too tired to keep talking. What makes her account consequential is what she reports he said. He said he had been driving fourteen hours. He said he was on a Streak Bonus run. He said the company's framing of drivers as neighbors made him feel patronized while the bonus structure was the thing actually shaping his shift. The police report, leaked this afternoon, lists driver fatigue as the contributing factor and confirms the fourteen-hour timeline through Daniel's statement to officers at the scene.

    One detail worth flagging. Greco's published founding story, repeated in the company's offering documents, names her first RideNet ride in Isla Vista in 2018 as the moment the idea crystallized. The driver was a man named Daniel. The Daniel V. at the center of tonight's story is the same person, now driving in metro markets on the platform he helped inspire.

    He said all of this from the front seat of a car operated by a company about to go public on the promise that its drivers feel differently.

    I have spent the last six hours speaking with current and former RideNet drivers in New York, Atlanta, Chicago, and Boston. Four of them agreed to be quoted. Their accounts converge on a single observation: the brand story RideNet has been telling investors is not the brand story drivers experience in the markets that account for most of the company's growth.

    Aisha Patel, a New York driver who is on the Streak Bonus tier regularly, told me she has been waiting for someone to say what Daniel said. "The bonus structure is the model. We chase the streak because we have to. The neighbor thing is something the company puts on top of what we already do." Ray Diaz, a former RideNet driver now at a competitor in Atlanta, said he left RideNet not because of the platform but because the marketing made him feel dishonest to passengers. "I am not their neighbor. I am their driver. There is a difference and the company knows there is a difference."

    The substantive question is whether the company's brand promise can survive its own scale. Naomi Tate, a sociologist at Cornell whose research on platform working conditions has documented patterns consistent with what Daniel describes, told me the story is the empirical case study she has been arguing should exist. "What he describes is what my work has been describing in journal articles for three years. The difference is that you can now point to a specific driver, a specific shift, and a police report that documents what the conditions actually produced."

    RideNet's response, transmitted to me just before deadline, says the company is reviewing the incident and remains committed to the working conditions of its driver community. It does not address the substance of what Daniel said. It does not respond to the question about the Streak Bonus structure. It does not acknowledge the gap between the metropolitan markets, where Daniel and Patel work, and the college town markets that built the brand.

    Pricing for the company's public offering is scheduled for next week. As of this evening two pension funds have withdrawn from the book. Senior equity analyst Mitchell Reinhardt lowered his price target by twenty-eight percent on Bloomberg this afternoon and said pricing on schedule is unlikely. The California Public Utilities Commission issued a public request for information at five fourteen this evening regarding driver classification practices and the accuracy of representations in the offering documents.

    The crash is a single moment in a single car. The conversation it has produced is about whether a company can sell its drivers a story while telling investors a different one. Daniel V. has not commented further. His phone, according to his cousin who answered for him, has not stopped ringing since noon.
